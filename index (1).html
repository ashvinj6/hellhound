Delta Blues · Dallas, Texas · 1937

Hellhound on My Trail

Robert Johnson, voice and guitar

Two and a half minutes of a man who can't stop moving, recorded by a musician who would be dead a year later. Many listeners consider it the most haunting record in the blues.

Vocalion Hellhound on My Trail Robert Johnson

The Story

A song about running

Robert Johnson spent most of his short life on the move. He drifted between Mississippi, Arkansas, Tennessee and beyond, playing juke joints, street corners and fish fries, rarely staying anywhere long enough to be known. "Hellhound on My Trail" turns that restlessness into something darker. The narrator can't rest because something is following him, and the song never says exactly what it is.

That ambiguity is the point. The hellhound can be read as guilt, a jealous rival, the law, a broken relationship, or something supernatural. In the years after Johnson's death, it became wrapped up in the legend that he sold his soul to the devil at a crossroads in exchange for his talent, and the song is often heard as the bill coming due.

The Session

Dallas, June 1937

Johnson recorded only twice in his life: once in San Antonio in November 1936, and once in Dallas in June 1937. "Hellhound on My Trail" came from the second session, which was the last time he ever recorded.

Roughly fourteen months later, in August 1938, Johnson died near Greenwood, Mississippi, at 27. The circumstances are still debated; the most repeated account says he was poisoned at a juke joint. He left behind 29 songs, and this one is often heard as his self-portrait.

Themes

What's chasing him

The lyrics are short and the images are stark. They don't tell a story so much as describe a state of mind: someone who can't stop, can't sleep and can't shake what's behind him.

Pursuit

The title image sets everything up. A hound tracks its prey by scent, so you can't outrun it, you can only stay ahead of it for a while.

Restlessness

The narrator keeps moving and the weather keeps turning against him. Travel here isn't freedom; it's the only option left.

Hoodoo

The song draws on Southern folk magic, including the belief that a scorned lover can sprinkle a powder to make someone wander forever.

Loneliness

Underneath the dread is a simple wish for home, comfort and someone to hold. It never comes.

The Sound

Fragile on purpose

Musically, the song owes a debt to Skip James's 1931 recording "Devil Got My Woman," which shares its eerie, minor-leaning feel and high, keening vocal. Johnson takes that template somewhere more unsettled.

His guitar part leans on an open tuning that gives the chords a hollow, unresolved sound, and he lets his voice crack upward into a near-falsetto. The rhythm drags and rushes, almost stumbling, as if the performer himself is out of breath. Few blues records of the era sound this nervous.

It doesn't sound like a man singing about fear. It sounds like a man who's afraid while the tape is rolling.

Legacy

Following the trail

The original single sold modestly. Johnson's reputation grew almost entirely after his death, and this song grew with it.

  1. 1931

    Skip James records "Devil Got My Woman," the song most often cited as Johnson's model.

  2. 1937

    Johnson records "Hellhound on My Trail" in Dallas at his final session. Vocalion releases it as a 78.

  3. 1938

    Johnson dies in Mississippi at 27. Producer John Hammond, unaware, had been trying to find him for the From Spirituals to Swing concert at Carnegie Hall.

  4. 1961

    Columbia releases the LP King of the Delta Blues Singers, which includes the song and introduces Johnson to a new generation of listeners and guitarists.

  5. 1960s+

    British and American blues-rock musicians cite Johnson as a foundational influence, and the hellhound becomes a recurring image across blues, rock and literature.
